Overview

As Payload Division Lead on Firehorn 1, I owned the complete 3-camera flight recording system from PCB design to mechanical mounts, and brought an acoustic levitation experiment from proof of concept to flight-ready hardware. Firehorn 1 flew at EuRoC 2025 as Switzerland's first cryogenic bi-liquid rocket.

Firehorn 1 and the Payload Division

Firehorn 1 is the EPFL Rocket Team's first EthaLOX (ethanol and liquid oxygen) single-stage launch vehicle. Designed for a 30 km apogee ceiling, its inaugural mission at EuRoC 2025 flew the 3 km qualification profile: 5 m tall, 83 kg dry mass, 7 kN thrust, 240 mm diameter. It is the first Swiss cryogenic bi-liquid rocket to have flown.

The Payload Division delivers two payloads: a 3U acoustic levitation demonstrator housed in the nose cone, and a three-camera flight recording system distributed along the airframe. I joined as Division Lead after a first-year student had completed a proof of concept for the acoustic levitation experiment, and built the camera system from scratch.

3-Camera Flight Recording System
Camera Selection and Communication

Three RunCam Split 4 V2 cameras were selected: compact at 10.2 g, capable of 2.7K at 60 fps with a 140° field of view, and physically separating the lens from the processing board so the lens can be positioned independently within tight aerocover geometry.

The RunCam communicates over UART, while the avionics use I2C converted to an I2CAN bus for long cable runs through the airframe. Each camera module therefore needs a protocol bridge and regulated power. The full chain is: Avionics MCU → I2C → LT3960 (I2CAN transceiver) → I2CAN → Custom PCB → UART → RunCam.

PCB Design

The camera PCB handles three functions: I2CAN to UART protocol conversion, a 24 V to 5 V step-down for the RunCam processing board, and camera on/off commands from the avionics. It was designed entirely in KiCad.

The prototyping phase used an Arduino Nano as the microcontroller, which made it easy to iterate on the firmware and validate the communication chain. Once the design was confirmed and space inside the aerocover became a hard constraint, I replaced the Nano with a Seeed XIAO: a far more compact board with the same serial capability, freeing up significant board area and mass budget. The board connects to the avionics rod via MPS connectors for modular integration.

  • LT3960 I2CAN transceiver for long-distance avionics communication
  • MPM3620 step-down converter (24 V to 5 V)
  • Seeed XIAO microcontroller replacing the Arduino Nano prototype
  • Photoresistor for liftoff detection on the camera power-on sequence
  • Aluminium thermal block bonded to each PCB for heat dissipation
Camera Mechanical Mounts

Three custom supports position each camera module in the airframe. The design constraints differ between positions: Camera 1 sits in a confined bottom aerocover and must face downward for liftoff monitoring, Camera 2 is radially oriented in the top aerocover and must survive the highest vibration loads, and Camera 3 mounts inside the avionics bay pointing at the parachute.

  • Camera 1: integrated into the bottom aerocover structure, facing down. 125.5 g. Modified aerocover geometry to clear the lens field of view.
  • Camera 2: top aerocover, radial orientation. Aluminium heat block for thermal capacity, spring-loaded screws to isolate the camera from structural vibration. 221.66 g.
  • Camera 3: avionics bay, fixed to the MPS rod stack, pointed at the parachute bay for deployment footage.
EuRoC 2025 and the SD Cards

Firehorn 1 lifted off at EuRoC 2025 and completed its flight, making history as Switzerland's first cryogenic bi-liquid rocket to fly. On landing, an unexpected fire broke out in the avionics bay. The heat burned through the electronics and melted the plastic enclosures around the camera modules.

All footage was lost. The SD cards were physically recovered from the debris but the thermal damage had corrupted the recorded data. Nothing was saved from any of the three cameras.

3U Experimental Payload
Acoustic Levitation Experiment

The 3U acoustic levitation payload uses two arrays of 19 ultrasonic transducers to generate a precise standing acoustic field capable of levitating a small spherical object. The aim is to observe how acoustic levitation behaves in high-g and low-g environments during flight.

A first-year student had completed a proof of concept before I joined the team. My role was to supervise and lead the work required to make it flight-ready: we re-soldered connections, replaced the driver and battery system with more reliable components, and ran extensive testing under vibration to confirm stable levitation before integration.
